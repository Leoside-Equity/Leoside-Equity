How a report is written

What gets covered

Three markets on a fixed weekly calendar: companies listed on the NYSE or the Nasdaq from Monday to Wednesday, companies listed in London on Thursday and Friday, and the Indian market over the weekend. The current week is laid out on the home page.

American and British reports each examine one listed company. Indian reports examine the market as a whole or a single sector, and give no view on any individual Indian listed security; the section on market and sector reports explains why.

Every company report asks the same four questions in the same order: what the accounts show, what protects the business, what the price assumes, and what could change the conclusion. A fixed order means two reports can be read side by side, and it makes a weak step easier to find.

The numbers

The first question is what the accounts actually show, before any opinion about the business is formed.

Revenue is broken down by product, customer and region wherever the filings allow, and each part is judged on how dependable it has been. Income that recurs, such as long contracts or repeat purchases, is treated differently from income that has to be won again every year.

Profit is then set against cash. A company can report rising operating profit while collecting less cash, for example when customers are given longer to pay, when unsold stock builds up, or when costs are capitalised on the balance sheet. Operating profit is compared with operating cash flow, and free cash flow with reported earnings, across several years. A gap that persists is treated as a question the report has to answer.

The balance sheet comes last: how much the company owes and when it falls due, how comfortably its interest is covered, how much cash it can reach, and whether the capital it employs earns more than that capital costs.

The business

The second question is what protects those numbers from competition.

Pricing power is judged from the record: whether the company has raised prices without losing customers, and what happened to its margins when it tried. Barriers to entry are considered one at a time (switching costs, network effects, regulation, scale, patents and brands), together with whether each is growing stronger or wearing away.

Management is judged by what it has done with surplus cash over several years. Money put into the business, spent on acquisitions, or returned through dividends and buybacks is compared with what each use went on to earn.

The price

The third question is what the current share price already assumes, and how that compares with an estimate of value built from the first two steps.

The main tool is a discounted cash flow model, which values a company as the cash it is expected to produce in future years, with each year's cash reduced to its value today at a rate that reflects the risk of receiving it. The model gives a range of values, and the report states the assumptions that move that range the most.

Where it helps, the model is also run in reverse. Starting from the market price, it asks what growth and margins the company would need to deliver to justify that price, which shows how demanding the market's expectations are.

As a check, the company is compared with its peers and with its own history on measures such as enterprise value to EBITDA and price to earnings relative to growth.

The outcome is recorded as a valuation stance: Undervalued, Fairly valued or Overvalued. The stance describes where the price stood against the range of values on the date of writing. It is an observation about price and never an instruction to buy, sell or hold, and the research disclaimer sets out exactly what each label means.

What could change it

The last question is what would move the argument, in either direction.

Some changes arrive on a known date: results, product launches, regulatory decisions, refinancing, changes to an index. Others build slowly, such as margins drifting, market share being won or lost, or the cost of capital rising or falling. The report names those that matter most to its conclusion.

Every company report also states plainly, in its body, the risks that would make it wrong.

Reading the key figures

A company report opens with a row of figures. They are defined the same way in every report.

Valuation stance
Where the market price stood against the author's range of values on the date of writing: below it (Undervalued), close to it (Fairly valued) or above it (Overvalued). It does not mean buy, hold or sell.
Fair value band
The range of values the model produces from the assumptions stated in the report. It is an estimate of what the business is worth. It does not forecast where the share price will go.
Last price at writing
The market price the report worked from, as it stood when the report was written. It is not updated afterwards.
Horizon
The period the assumptions were framed around, such as 12 to 24 months. It says nothing about when, or whether, the price will move.
Market
The country whose exchange lists the company, which also sets the currency the figures are given in.

Indian market and sector reports carry none of these figures.

Market and sector reports

The Indian weekend reports ask the same questions of many companies at once. Saturday takes one sector: what is driving its earnings, where the risk is concentrated, and the strongest case on each side. Sunday looks at the market as a whole through the indices, interest rates, inflation, the rupee and the flow of money into and out of Indian shares.

These reports carry no valuation stance, fair value band or price horizon, and they do not recommend or rate any individual Indian listed security. In India, research on listed securities is regulated by the Securities and Exchange Board of India, and Leoside Equity is not registered with it.

Sources

Annual and interim reports, regulatory filings, investor presentations, earnings call transcripts, central bank and statistics office releases, and publicly available market data.

Where a figure comes from a particular document, the report says which. Facts are kept apart from estimates and opinions, and projections are labelled as projections.

Corrections

When a report gets something factually wrong, a correction goes at the top of the report with the date and what changed. Errors are not edited out quietly. A change of opinion is not a correction: a report stands as a record of the view held on its date. To report an error, write to support@leosideequity.com.

Terms used in the reports

The measures the reports use most, in plain terms.

Free cash flow
The cash a business generates after paying for what it must spend to maintain and grow itself. It is the money available to repay debt or return to shareholders.
Operating margin
Operating profit, also called EBIT (earnings before interest and tax), as a share of revenue.
Discounted cash flow
A valuation that adds up the cash a business is expected to produce in future years, with each year's cash reduced to what it is worth today.
Reverse discounted cash flow
The same model worked backwards from the current share price, to find the growth and margins that the price implies.
Cost of capital
The return that lenders and shareholders together require for providing money to a company, often written as WACC (weighted average cost of capital). It is the rate used to discount future cash.
Terminal growth
The rate at which cash is assumed to grow for ever after the years that are forecast in detail. A small change to it can move a valuation a long way.
Enterprise value to EBITDA
The value of the whole business (its shares plus its net debt) divided by earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. It allows companies with different amounts of debt to be compared.
Price to earnings
The share price divided by earnings per share: how many years of current profit the market is paying for.
Multiple compression
A fall in the multiple the market is willing to pay, such as price to earnings, which can lower a share price even when profit holds steady.
Return on capital employed
Operating profit as a share of the capital a business uses. Set against the cost of capital, it shows whether growth adds value or uses it up.

Limits

A consistent method does not make a report right. Every step rests on assumptions that can fail, and a careful process still produces conclusions that turn out wrong. Nothing produced this way is personal advice. Read the research disclaimer before relying on any report.
